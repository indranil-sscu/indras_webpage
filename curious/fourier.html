How to use.
  1. Choose a target waveform.
  2. Increase the number of harmonics and watch the sum (solid line) approach the target (dashed).
  3. Each circle spins at a whole-number multiple of the slowest one; the height of the last circle's tip traces the wave. Pause or change the speed at any time.

What is going on?

A Fourier series writes a repeating signal as a sum of sine and cosine waves whose frequencies are whole-number multiples (harmonics) of the basic repetition rate. Each term is a circle turning at its own speed. Add the circles tip to tail and the height of the last tip traces the signal.

The spectrum (bottom plot) shows how much of each harmonic is needed. A square wave uses only odd harmonics, with amplitudes falling as 1/k. A triangle wave's fall much faster, as 1/k², because it has no jumps, so a few harmonics already look right.

Near a jump the sum always overshoots by about 9% of the jump, however many harmonics you add: the Gibbs phenomenon. The overshoot gets narrower but never smaller. This is why sharp edges in signals and images ring after filtering.

Fourier analysis is everywhere: in music and audio compression, in MRI scanners, and in spectrometers like the ones in our lab, which measure signals against time and Fourier-transform them into colours. For sampling and FFT axes in the lab, see the FFT axis designer.

The mathematics
f(x) = a₀/2 + Σk≥1 [ak cos kx + bk sin kx],   ak = (1/π)∫ f(x) cos kx dx,   bk = (1/π)∫ f(x) sin kx dx
square: bk = 4/(πk), odd k;   sawtooth: bk = 2(−1)k+1/(πk);   triangle: bk = 8(−1)(k−1)/2/(π²k²), odd k;   pulse (width d): ak = 2 sin(πkd)/(πk)
Parseval: ⟨f²⟩ = (a₀/2)² + ½ Σ (ak² + bk²);   Gibbs: max SK → (2/π) Si(π) ≈ 1.179 for a ±1 square wave

Each circle has radius √(ak² + bk²) and turns k times per period. The coefficients are exact formulas, checked by automated tests against numerical integration, and the tests also confirm the Gibbs limit.
