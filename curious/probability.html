What is going on?

Probability tells you what fraction of many repeated trials will give each outcome. One roll of a fair die is unpredictable, but in many rolls each face turns up about 1/6 of the time.

Sums of dice are not equally likely. With two dice there is one way to make 2 (1 + 1) but six ways to make 7 (1+6, 2+5, 3+4, 4+3, 5+2, 6+1), so 7 is six times as likely as 2. With more dice the distribution becomes a smooth bell curve, an example of the central limit theorem.

The law of large numbers says that the average of many trials approaches the expected value. The shaded band shows where the running average is expected to be (±2 standard deviations); it narrows as 1/√N, so a hundred times more rolls make your estimate ten times more precise.

The gambler's fallacy. A coin has no memory: after five heads in a row the next toss is still 50 : 50. Long streaks are normal. In N fair tosses the longest run is typically about log₂ N long (about 10 in a thousand tosses).

The mathematics
P(sum = s) for n dice with m faces = coefficient of xs in ((x + x² + … + xm)/m)n   (computed exactly by convolution)
E[sum] = n(m + 1)/2,   Var[sum] = n(m² − 1)/12,   running mean ≈ E ± 2√(Var/N)
P(k heads in 10 tosses) = C(10, k) pk(1 − p)10−k   (binomial distribution)

Random numbers come from a fast pseudo-random generator (mulberry32). The calculations are checked by automated tests against the exact formulas above.
