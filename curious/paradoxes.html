1. The birthday paradox

How to use.
  1. Set the number of people in the room. Press New room to give everyone a random birthday; people who share a day are highlighted on the year wheel.
  2. Press Simulate rooms to fill thousands of rooms of that size and count how often at least two people share a birthday.
  3. Simulate every size runs rooms of every size at once. The dots (simulation) should sit on the curve (exact formula).

Controls

One room

Why so few people are enough

It feels as if you would need about 183 people (half of 365) for a shared birthday to be likely. But the question is not whether someone shares your birthday; it is whether any two people share one. With 23 people there are 23 × 22 / 2 = 253 pairs, and every pair is a chance for a match.

It is easiest to count the opposite. The second person avoids the first person's birthday with probability 364/365, the third avoids both with 363/365, and so on. Multiply these together for everyone in the room and subtract from 1. With 23 people the chance that all birthdays differ has dropped to 49.3%, so a shared birthday is slightly more likely than not. With 57 people it is 99%.

The same mathematics tells computer scientists how many random identifiers can be issued before two collide (a birthday attack on a hash function needs about the square root of the number of possible values).

The mathematics
P(shared) = 1 − ∏k=0n−1 (d − k)/d = 1 − d! / ((d − n)! dn),   d = 365
P(shared) ≈ 1 − exp(−n(n − 1)/2d),   so P = ½ near n ≈ √(2d ln 2) ≈ 22.5
expected number of matching pairs = C(n, 2)/d = 253/365 ≈ 0.69 for n = 23

Assumptions: birthdays are independent and equally likely, and 29 February is ignored. Real birthdays are slightly uneven through the year, which only makes a match more likely. The simulation draws each birthday from a seeded pseudo-random generator; automated tests check the exact values (P(23) = 0.507297…) and that simulated rooms agree with them within the statistical error.

2. Simpson's paradox

How to use.
  1. Pick a data set. Each treatment's success rate is shown for each group and overall; the higher rate in each column is highlighted.
  2. Edit any number in the table (successes and cases), or drag the mix slider to move one treatment's cases between the groups while keeping its success rate in each group.
  3. Watch the chart: each treatment's overall rate is a point on the line joining its two group rates, placed according to its mix of cases.

Controls

The table

How a trend can reverse

In the kidney-stone study, open surgery did better than keyhole surgery for small stones and for large stones, yet keyhole surgery had the better overall record. Nothing is miscounted. The overall rate is an average of the group rates, weighted by how many cases fell in each group, and the two treatments had very different mixes: open surgery was mostly given to the hard, large-stone cases.

In the chart, each line runs from a treatment's rate in the first group (left) to its rate in the second group (right). Its overall rate must lie on its own line, at the position set by its share of second-group cases. If one line is higher everywhere but its point sits far to the low side, the points can swap order. Drag the mix slider until both treatments have the same mix and the paradox disappears.

Which answer should you trust? It depends on why the groups differ. Here stone size affected both the choice of treatment and the outcome (a confounder), so the within-group comparison is the fair one. With other causal structures the aggregate can be the right answer, which is why the paradox is a lesson about causes, not arithmetic.

The mathematics
pA = (1 − wA) pA1 + wA pA2,   wA = nA2/(nA1 + nA2), and likewise for B
reversal: pA1 > pB1 and pA2 > pB2, yet pA < pB  (possible only if wA ≠ wB)

If both treatments have the same mix (wA = wB), the overall comparison always agrees with the group comparisons. The chart is the construction of Baker and Kramer. Automated tests check the published data sets and the weighted-average identity.
