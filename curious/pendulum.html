How to use.
  1. Choose a simple or a double pendulum. Drag a bob with the mouse or a finger to set where it starts, or use the angle sliders, then let go.
  2. Simple pendulum: change the length, gravity or release angle and compare the measured period with the textbook formula 2π√(L/g), which is exact only for small swings.
  3. Double pendulum: switch on the twin, started a thousandth of a degree away, and watch the two separate. The chart shows how well the simulation conserves energy; try a larger time step.

What is going on?

A simple pendulum is a weight on a light rod. Gravity pulls it back towards the bottom with a force proportional to sin θ. For small swings sin θ ≈ θ, the motion is a pure sine wave, and the period is 2π√(L/g): it depends on the length and on gravity, but not on the mass or on how far you pull it. That is why pendulums made good clocks. For larger swings the period grows: about 1.7% longer at 30°, 18% at 90°. The dashed curve on the chart is the small-angle prediction; release the pendulum from 90° and watch the real motion fall behind it.

A double pendulum obeys equally simple laws, yet for large swings it is chaotic: tiny differences in the start grow exponentially until the two motions have nothing in common. Turn on the twin, started 0.001° away. For a few seconds the two overlap perfectly, then they separate. Long-term prediction is impossible in practice, even though nothing random is involved. This sensitivity is what limits weather forecasts to about two weeks.

The computer cannot solve these equations exactly. It steps forward in time with the fourth-order Runge–Kutta method (RK4), whose error per step shrinks as the fifth power of the step. Because there is no friction, the total energy should stay constant, so the drift in energy measures the numerical error. At the default 2 ms step it stays below one part in a million; at 50 ms it becomes visible.

The mathematics
simple:   θ̈ = −(g/L) sin θ − γ θ̇,   small angles: T₀ = 2π√(L/g),   with damping: T = 2π/√(g/L − γ²/4)
any amplitude θ₀:   T = 4√(L/g) K(sin(θ₀/2)) = T₀ / AGM(1, cos(θ₀/2))   (K: complete elliptic integral; AGM: arithmetic–geometric mean)
double (Δ = θ₁ − θ₂, D = 2m₁ + m₂ − m₂ cos 2Δ):
θ̈₁ = [−g(2m₁ + m₂) sin θ₁ − m₂g sin(θ₁ − 2θ₂) − 2 sin Δ · m₂(θ̇₂²L₂ + θ̇₁²L₁ cos Δ)] / (L₁D)
θ̈₂ = 2 sin Δ [θ̇₁²L₁(m₁ + m₂) + g(m₁ + m₂) cos θ₁ + θ̇₂²L₂m₂ cos Δ] / (L₂D)
E = ½m₁L₁²θ̇₁² + ½m₂[L₁²θ̇₁² + L₂²θ̇₂² + 2L₁L₂θ̇₁θ̇₂ cos Δ] + (m₁ + m₂)gL₁(1 − cos θ₁) + m₂gL₂(1 − cos θ₂)
RK4: yn+1 = yn + (h/6)(k₁ + 2k₂ + 2k₃ + k₄), global error ∝ h⁴

The period is measured from the simulation by timing successive upward passes through the bottom, interpolated between steps. Automated tests check that RK4 converges at fourth order, that the measured period matches 2π√(L/g) for small swings and the elliptic-integral value for large ones, that damping removes energy as e−γt, that the double pendulum's small-oscillation frequencies equal √((2 ∓ √2)g/L), and that its relative energy drift over 30 s at the default step stays below 10⁻³.
