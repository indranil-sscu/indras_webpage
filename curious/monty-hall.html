How to play.
  1. Click (or tab to and press Enter on) a door to pick it.
  2. The host, who knows where the car is, opens the other doors except one, always revealing goats.
  3. Decide: stay with your door or switch to the one still closed. Play a few rounds, then press Simulate to run thousands of games.

The game

Why switching wins

When you first pick, your door hides the car with probability 1/3, so the car is behind one of the other two doors with probability 2/3. The host then opens one of those two, but he always opens a goat: he knows where the car is and never reveals it. Opening it tells you nothing new about your own door, which stays at 1/3. The whole 2/3 now sits on the one door he left closed.

Put another way: switching loses only if your first pick was right (1 time in 3). Staying wins only in that same case.

With n doors, where the host opens all but one of the others, staying wins 1/n of the time and switching (n − 1)/n. With 10 doors, switching wins 90% of the time, which makes the logic easier to feel.

The mathematics (Bayes' theorem)
Let Ci = “car behind door i”, you picked door 1, host opened door 3 (H3).
P(H3 | C1) = ½,   P(H3 | C2) = 1,   P(H3 | C3) = 0
P(C2 | H3) = P(H3 | C2) P(C2) / Σi P(H3 | Ci) P(Ci) = (1 · ⅓) / (½ · ⅓ + 1 · ⅓ + 0) = 2/3

Assumptions that matter: the host always opens a door, never reveals the car, and always offers the switch. If he opened a door at random and happened to show a goat, the odds would be 50 : 50. The simulation uses the standard rules; the automated tests check that it reproduces 1/n and (n − 1)/n.
