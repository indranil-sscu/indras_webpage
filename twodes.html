How to use.
  1. Pick a response function on the left: a built-in model (start from an example), your own formula, or measured data as CSV.
  2. Choose what to look at above the map (rephasing, non-rephasing, absorptive; real, imaginary, magnitude, phase) and move the waiting time T.
  3. Set the time-domain sampling, zero padding, window and units on the right. Click the map to read a value, take slices and compute the waiting-time trace at that point. Use Check convergence before trusting fine details.

          Understanding the mathematics

          Response functions, pathways and 2D spectra

          The third-order response. Three short pulses, separated by the coherence time t₁ and the waiting (population) time T, drive a polarization that radiates during the detection time t₃. In the impulsive limit the radiated signal is the third-order response function, a sum of nested commutators of the dipole operator:

          R(3)(t₃, T, t₁) = (i/ħ)³ θ(t₃)θ(T)θ(t₁) ⟨[[[μ(t₁ + T + t₃), μ(t₁ + T)], μ(t₁)], μ(0)] ρeq⟩ = Σpathways ± Rα

          Why three intervals? During t₁ the system is in an optical coherence (a superposition of ground and excited states), which oscillates at the transition frequency. The second pulse turns it into a population, or into a coherence between two excited states, which evolves during T. The third pulse creates a new optical coherence that radiates during t₃. The first and last intervals therefore label which frequency was excited and which was detected. T tracks what happened in between: relaxation, energy transfer, vibrational or electronic coherence.

          Rephasing and non-rephasing pathways. Phase matching selects terms by their wavevector. In the rephasing direction kR = −k₁ + k₂ + k₃ the coherence evolves as e+iωt₁ and then e−iωt₃, so dephasing from static disorder is undone at t₃ = t₁: a photon echo. In the non-rephasing direction kNR = +k₁ − k₂ + k₃ both intervals evolve with the same sign and no echo forms. The two must be computed separately; one response cannot simply be used for both.

          two-level system:   RR = A e+iωegt₁ − Γt₁ e−iωegt₃ − Γt₃ e−T/T₁,   RNR = A e−iωegt₁ − Γt₁ e−iωegt₃ − Γt₃ e−T/T₁

          Why Fourier transform along t₁ and t₃. Transforming the two coherence times turns oscillation frequencies into peak positions: excitation frequency ω₁ and detection frequency ω₃. T is kept as a parameter, so a 2D spectrum is a correlation map, "excite here, see the response there", at each waiting time:

          SR(ω₁, T, ω₃) = ∫₀∞dt₁ ∫₀∞dt₃ RR e−iω₁t₁ e+iω₃t₃,   SNR = ∫∫ RNR e+iω₁t₁ e+iω₃t₃,   Sabs = Re[SR + SNR]

          Each one-sided transform of e−Γt gives a complex Lorentzian with an absorptive real part and a dispersive imaginary part. In the rephasing spectrum alone the dispersive parts mix in and the peaks look "phase twisted"; adding the non-rephasing spectrum cancels them, leaving purely absorptive peaks: 2Γ/(Γ² + Δ₁²) · Γ/(Γ² + Δ₃²) for a two-level system.

          Diagonal and cross peaks. A peak on the diagonal (ω₁ = ω₃) means the transition that was excited also responded. A cross peak at (ωa, ωb) means that exciting a changed the response at b: the transitions share a ground state, are coupled, or exchange energy. Two independent transitions give no cross peaks, because their cross-peak pathways (GSB, SE and ESA) cancel exactly; example 2 shows this. Coupling breaks the cancellation (example 3). Excited-state absorption gives negative features where the doubly excited state absorbs.

          Homogeneous and inhomogeneous broadening. Fast fluctuations (homogeneous dephasing Γ) broaden a peak equally in all directions: a round peak of FWHM 2Γ. A static spread of transition frequencies (inhomogeneous broadening) stretches the peak along the diagonal, because each molecule is excited and detected at its own frequency. The anti-diagonal width still reports the homogeneous linewidth: the rephasing echo removes the static spread. As the waiting time grows, spectral diffusion would make the peak rounder; this is a planned extension.

          • ωT is evaluated by a direct Fourier sum at the chosen frequency, which equals arbitrarily fine zero padding (the resolution is still 2π/ΔT ≈ 30 cm⁻¹ for a 1.1 ps window).

          Coherence maps

          Vibrational wavepackets make 2D peaks oscillate with the waiting time T. A coherence map (CM) collects these oscillations as a 2D map at one beat frequency ωT.

          1. Compute the rephasing spectrum SR(ω₁, T, ω₃) for many T.
          2. Remove the slowly varying population signal with a global fit, a·e−T/τ₁ + b·e−T/τ₂ + c. The time constants are shared by all pixels; the amplitudes are fitted per pixel.
          3. Fourier transform the residual along T.

          The real rephasing CM transforms Re SR, so beats at ±ωT are added and can interfere. Transforming the complex SR separates the two beat phases. In the rephasing signal, ground-state (GSB) vibrational coherences at the diagonal peak oscillate with the opposite phase to excited-state (ESE) ones (Sahu & Tiwari label them −ωT and +ωT). Where they overlap with similar amplitude they cancel in the real part above and below the diagonal, giving a narrow, node-like diagonal lineshape that disappears in the separated ±ωT maps.

          CMreal(ω₁, ω₃; ωT) = |ΣT rRe(T) e−iωTT ΔT|,   CM± = |ΣT r(T) e±iωTT ΔT|   (r: residual of the complex SR)

          The beat spectrum under the map (summed over the displayed region) shows which frequencies are present; click it to choose ωT.

          Conventions and numerical methods

          Sign conventions. The built-in models use the E ∝ e−iωt convention: RR ∝ e+iωat₁e−iωbt₃ and RNR ∝ e−iωat₁e−iωbt₃. The transform kernels are selectable. The standard choice flips the rephasing ω₁ axis so that both pathways appear at positive frequencies. The unflipped choice shows the rephasing signal at −ω₁. The conjugate choice is for data written with e+iωt. Changing a kernel sign mirrors that frequency axis; the tests check this exactly. The absorptive spectrum is Re[SR + SNR], in which ground-state bleach and stimulated emission are positive and excited-state absorption negative. The factor i of the emitted field and the local-oscillator phase are absorbed into this convention.

          Rotating frame. Optical frequencies (around 3 rad/fs) would need sub-femtosecond sampling. The response is therefore sampled in a frame rotating at the carrier ωc: Rrot = Rlab·e−iωct₁+iωct₃ (rephasing) and Rlab·e+iωct₁+iωct₃ (non-rephasing). The frequency axes are shifted back by ωc. Only frequencies inside ωc ± π/Δt are represented; anything outside aliases, and the simulator warns when a model transition does.

          Discrete transform. S(ω₁, ω₃) ≈ Δt² Σ wj wk R(jΔt, kΔt) ei s₁ω₁jΔt + i s₃ω₃kΔt by a radix-2 FFT, with ω = ωc + 2πm/(NΔt) after the FFT shift. The t = 0 samples are halved (trapezoid rule). Without this the one-sided transform acquires a constant offset that distorts the absorptive spectrum. Nonuniformly sampled data are transformed by a direct nonuniform Fourier sum with trapezoid weights, or, if you choose, resampled to a uniform grid; uniform sampling is never assumed silently.

          Zero padding is not resolution. Padding to N points makes the displayed grid finer (2π/(NΔt)) by interpolation. The physical resolution is set by the sampled window, about 2π/tmax, and by the linewidths. A truncated response (signal not decayed by tmax) gives sinc ripples; a window removes them at the cost of extra broadening. The numerical broadening option multiplies by e−γt/2 on both axes, adding a Lorentzian of FWHM γ. Check convergence recomputes with Δt/2 and with 2tmax and reports how much the spectrum changes in the displayed region.

          Validation. Automated tests (tests/twodes-core.test.js) check:

          • a single transition peaks at (ωeg, ωeg), and its width is 2Γ on both axes
          • the signal decays as e−T/T₁
          • the rephasing and non-rephasing peak positions follow each convention, and static disorder elongates the rephasing peak along the diagonal
          • the absorptive spectrum equals the product of Lorentzians to 0.2%
          • zero padding leaves the linewidth unchanged
          • the FFT equals a direct Fourier sum to 10⁻¹⁰
          • flipping a convention mirrors the axis
          • the J = 0 dimer equals two independent transitions, and the electronic beat period is 2π/|E₊ − E₋|
          • the vibronic model reduces to the two-level system at S = 0 and beats at 2π/ωv
          • the formula mode reproduces the built-in model
          • CSV export and import round-trip exactly

          Scope and limitations

          This is an impulsive-limit simulator: the pulses are treated as delta functions, so the spectra are those of the molecular response itself. A measured 2D spectrum also contains the pulse spectra and phases (finite-pulse convolution and windowing), the detection and phasing procedure, scatter, propagation and solvent effects, and the local-oscillator phase. Quantitative agreement with an experiment should not be expected unless those are included.

          Not included yet, and planned as separate modules:

          • finite-duration Gaussian pulses and their convolution with the response
          • spectral diffusion (time-dependent frequency correlation) beyond static disorder
          • population transfer between excitons
          • polarization schemes beyond ⟨ZZZZ⟩ and ⟨ZZYY⟩
          • double-quantum spectroscopy

          The models use Bloch (exponential) dephasing except the vibronic model, which treats one harmonic mode exactly within the cumulant expansion.
